# Story patterns catalog

Sticky moments → research-backed patterns → template books → structural
requirements. Used by `content/authoring-doctrine.md` when drafting a
custom book, and by `pnpm order:preview` to cite the pattern in the
buyer preview email.

If the intake's `sticky_moment` field matches one of the entries below
(fuzzy match on the phrasing), that entry's pattern is what the story
should use. If nothing matches cleanly, pick the closest neighbour and
note the adaptation in a `notes` line — do not default to a generic
adventure.

The patterns are drawn from `reference/storyverse/docs/07-therapeutic-techniques.md`
(narrative therapy fundamentals), `08-story-structure.md` (age-band
architectures), and the working library of existing Little Fables books
that already prove each pattern out.

---

## 1. Big feelings that come out as hitting / yelling / pushing

**Also phrased as:** tantrums, meltdowns, "he's a good kid but", dysregulation, "the switch flips fast".

**Pattern:** Externalization + body-based tool. The overwhelming feeling gets a *name and a shape* (a creature, a weather event, a "bigness") so the child can observe it instead of being it. The story hands them a three-step body-based regulation tool they can invoke in the actual next moment.

**Age range:** 3–7. For older children (7+) the tool can be more cognitive (self-talk, reframing) — see pattern §10.

**Reference:** `reference/storyverse/docs/07-therapeutic-techniques.md` — "Emotional Regulation Through Story" and "The Externalization Framework".

**Template book:** `content/originals/the_moose_who_knew_about_bigness_formatted.md`. The moose externalizes the child's aggression as "the bigness". Its scar is proof that even wise creatures once crashed through things they didn't mean to. The "Gentle Giant's Secret" — *stand still like a moose, breathe like the mountain, find the soft place behind your strong* — is the tool the family carries forward.

**Story must include:**
- A non-human mentor that has been where the child is (a scar, a memory, a shared "I know")
- A specific name for the overwhelming feeling (not just "big feelings" — *the bigness*, *the storm*, *the too-much*)
- A moment where the mentor separates the *child's identity* from the *behavior* ("your kindness is real; the bigness buries it sometimes")
- A three-step tool with sensory scaffolding (roots in the ground, breath like the mountain, hand on heart) — the exact same structure the parent guide teaches
- Callbacks that use the child's specific small kindnesses (from the intake's `interests` / `traits` / `look`) as evidence of who they really are
- Grace for imperfection: the tool doesn't erase the feeling, it makes it manageable. And you'll still forget sometimes. That's the point.

**Parent-guide tool:** The three-step secret with named body cues. Key phrase: *"the bigness came out — my hands did things my heart didn't mean."* This becomes the family's shared vocabulary for the next real incident.

## 2. Bedtime resistance / "stay with me" / separation anxiety at night

**Also phrased as:** won't fall asleep alone, needs a parent in the room, cries when we leave, wakes and calls.

**Pattern:** Progressive pacing arc (high → medium → low → still → sleep cues) with a *companion-relay comfort* structure — as the parent steps back, a trusted stand-in (a stuffed animal, a favourite object, the moon) takes over the "watching" role. The child isn't alone; the shape of who's watching just shifts.

**Age range:** 2–6.

**Reference:** `reference/storyverse/docs/07-therapeutic-techniques.md` — "Bedtime Stories: The Sleep Transition Framework" and "Addressing Specific Bedtime Challenges → Separation Anxiety".

**Template books:**
- `content/originals/the_cozy_circle_formatted.md` — Jujy takes "Night Watch" as Mama steps to just-outside-the-door. Explicit relay.
- `content/originals/papa_gets_the_moon_formatted.md` — Papa honours the ask by "getting" the moon; the moon becomes the sustained watching presence after Papa leaves.

**Story must include:**
- Progressive downregulation pacing: page 1–3 gentle activity → page 4–5 gathering / return → page 6–7 sensory comfort (warm, soft, dim) → page 8+ stillness with sleep cues (yawn, eyes heavy, dark-as-blanket)
- A named handoff moment — the parent doesn't just leave; the watching role is *explicitly transferred* to a stand-in the child trusts (stuffed animal, moon, night light, favourite blanket)
- Darkness described in positive sensory terms (soft, cozy, gentle, wrapped) — never "don't be scared"
- Tomorrow is promised concretely ("we'll have pancakes in the morning, just like always")
- The final page is stillness, not a punchline

**Parent-guide tool:** *The Handoff* — a script the parent uses at lights-out that names the stand-in and its job ("Jujy has Night Watch tonight. He'll be here the whole time. If you need me, I'm right down the hall."). Key phrase: *"who has Night Watch tonight?"*

## 3. Fear of the dark

**Also phrased as:** needs a nightlight, calls out that something's in the room, hides under blankets.

**Pattern:** Reframe darkness as a *presence*, not an absence — the dark is a soft blanket the world uses to rest; the moon and the night sounds are the child's company, not enemies.

**Age range:** 3–6.

**Reference:** `reference/storyverse/docs/07-therapeutic-techniques.md` — "Addressing Specific Bedtime Challenges → Fear of Dark".

**Template book:** `content/originals/the_coocoo_and_the_boy_who_could_formatted.md` — the house talks in creaks and settling sounds; the moon is patient and unbothered; the child learns his own trust in the dark.

**Story must include:**
- Darkness given warm sensory language every time it appears (velvet dark, soft dark, blanket dark)
- The night as *active company* — the moon watches, the house settles, the wind touches the window
- Sounds the child might already fear (creaks, wind) reframed as the house talking, doing its own small nighttime jobs
- A moment where the child notices they are *not scared right now* — quiet realization, no fanfare
- Nightlights and stuffed animal companions are normalized, not framed as babyish

**Parent-guide tool:** *Naming the night sounds* — a bedtime ritual where the parent and child name three sounds they hear and what job each sound is doing ("the fridge is humming its dinner song; the tree is stretching its arms"). Key phrase: *"what's the house telling us tonight?"*

## 4. Transitions / plans changing / rigidity

**Also phrased as:** melts down at the end of screen time, hard to switch activities, gets stuck on how things "should" be, upset by detours.

**Pattern:** Reframe the detour as a *discovery*. The story starts with the child's plan, disrupts it early, and the disruption becomes the good part — the character finds something they wouldn't have found on the planned route.

**Age range:** 3–8.

**Reference:** `reference/storyverse/docs/07-therapeutic-techniques.md` — "Adaptability & Flexibility"; also `reference/azi-verse/future-ready-skills.md` — SS003 Adaptive Resilience.

**Template book:** `content/originals/azi_bus_detour_formatted.md` — the bus route to school is closed; the group takes a new way and finds things they'd never seen.

**Story must include:**
- The plan is stated clearly and looked-forward-to on page 1 (so the disruption *lands*)
- The disruption arrives from outside the child's control (weather, closure, someone else's decision) — the child's job is not to accept it, but to *notice what's here that wouldn't have been*
- One specific "we wouldn't have found this if" moment — a small treasure, a new person, a view
- A companion who models the flexibility without preaching (the wise stuffed animal, the calm sibling) — the mentor is the *demonstration*, not the lesson
- The story ends back at the destination, but the child now holds both — the plan, and the something new

**Parent-guide tool:** *The wouldn't-have-found* — a repair phrase for the actual next tantrum-at-transition. "We wouldn't have gotten to [thing that's happening] if we'd stayed with the plan." Key phrase: *"what did the detour give us?"*

## 5. I hurt someone I love (repair after a big-feelings moment)

**Also phrased as:** hit their sibling, said mean things to grandma, feels terrible after but doesn't know how to say it.

**Pattern:** Repair modeling — the story shows the *shame beat* (the character's face after they realize what they did) and then walks through the concrete steps of coming back: naming what happened, saying the words, letting the loved one respond. Emphasis on repair being *possible*, not on shame being deserved.

**Age range:** 3–8.

**Reference:** `reference/storyverse/docs/07-therapeutic-techniques.md` — "Processing Difficult Life Events → Safe Distance Through Metaphor" (adapted for interpersonal repair); the moose book (pattern §1) is also relevant as a precursor.

**Template book:** `content/originals/azi_thunder_symphony_formatted.md` — Azi's frustration overflows and he hurts gentle Citie in the moment. The story doesn't skip over the shame; it walks through the return.

**Story must include:**
- The moment of harm shown without minimizing (a small clear injury — hurt feelings, a knocked-over friend, a shove)
- The *shame beat* — the child's face when they realize what they did, the cold heavy feeling, the wish they could take it back
- The loved one does not disappear, retaliate, or dismiss — they *stay*, quiet and steady (this models what safe attachment looks like)
- A specific repair script the child speaks: *"I did [what I did]. It wasn't your fault. I'm sorry. Can I [specific repair action]?"*
- The loved one accepts the repair without a lecture; the story does not moralize
- A callback the next day showing the relationship intact — they play, they eat together, the harm did not break them

**Parent-guide tool:** *The repair sentence* — a five-part script the child can use next time. Named parts (what I did / not your fault / sorry / can I). Key phrase: *"can we do a repair?"*

## 6. New sibling / feeling replaced

**Also phrased as:** regression after baby arrives, "when is the baby going home", needs extra reassurance.

**Pattern:** *Safe-distance metaphor* (the child is not the child in the story; a bear cub is the bear cub) + *ceremony of specialness* — a story that gives the older child a specific role, name, or gift that no one else can hold. Belonging is expanded, not divided.

**Age range:** 2–6.

**Reference:** `reference/storyverse/docs/07-therapeutic-techniques.md` — "Processing Difficult Life Events → Safe Distance Through Metaphor".

**Template book:** No current Little Fables book covers this well; adapt from the Moose (externalization of the "left out" feeling) plus the Cozy Circle (the reassurance-through-ritual shape). *We should author one when a real order calls for it.*

**Story must include:**
- The main character is *not* directly the buyer's child — a bear cub, a small wolf, a puppy. The child sees themselves through the character, not as them.
- The older character has a specific ability, role, or memory that the new arrival cannot yet share (they know all the good hiding spots; they can reach the honey; they remember what it was like before)
- A moment of "the small one gets more attention right now" is shown honestly — the character *feels* it, doesn't perform being fine
- A parent-figure in the story names the older child's specific place: *"you were the first — I learned how to be a mama by loving you"*
- The older character discovers something they can *give* the new one — a name for a toy, a first song, a first walked-to spot
- The story ends with both siblings together, not with the older one told to "share"

**Parent-guide tool:** *The specific ceremony* — a real-world ritual the parent invents that belongs only to the older child (their special "big kid" chair, a walk that's just theirs, a bedtime song from before). Key phrase: *"this is yours — from before."*

## 7. Fear of a specific thing (dogs, storms, doctors, water)

**Also phrased as:** freezes around dogs, hides during thunder, cries at the doctor.

**Pattern:** Gradual exposure through story. The character *observes from safety*, then observes closer, then makes a small approach — always with permission to stop. The story does not resolve the fear; it *shrinks the worry to a manageable size*.

**Age range:** 3–8.

**Reference:** `reference/storyverse/docs/07-therapeutic-techniques.md` — "Anxiety Management Through Story → The Gradual Exposure Framework".

**Template book:** `content/originals/azi_thunder_symphony_formatted.md` covers this partially (thunder reframed as music). For animals / doctors we'd draft new.

**Story must include:**
- The feared thing is present but *doing nothing threatening* — the dog is sitting; the thunder is loud but far; the doctor is smiling
- The character is *given explicit permission not to engage* by a trusted adult ("you can watch from here; no need to go closer")
- A small honest brave moment (three steps closer, a peek from behind Mama, one whispered "hi") that the character *chose*
- The fear is not gone at the end — it is *smaller*. "Still scared, but less."
- An adult names the courage without exaggerating ("standing closer was a big deal") — no "look how brave you are now!" energy
- Explicit note that "next time" is fine, no rush

**Parent-guide tool:** *The three steps closer* — a real-world exposure ladder the parent uses at the actual thing. "Today let's just watch from the window / from the bench / from ten feet away." Key phrase: *"how close do you want to be today?"*

## 8. Grief / loss / a hard life event (moving, divorce, death, illness)

**Also phrased as:** grandma died, we're moving, mama and papa live in different houses now, someone is sick.

**Pattern:** Safe-distance metaphor with *containment* — the story acknowledges the loss honestly but places it inside a container the child can hold (a season changing, a bird leaving the nest, a star that keeps shining). Never forces cheer; sits with the feeling.

**Age range:** 3–10. Handled with extra care for 3–5; more direct for 6–10.

**Reference:** `reference/storyverse/docs/07-therapeutic-techniques.md` — "Processing Difficult Life Events → Safe Distance Through Metaphor" (this is the most important pattern to follow doctrine on — the copy in that doc has explicit dos/don'ts).

**Template book:** Not yet in the library. *If a real order requires this, escalate — the story should be co-written with the parent, not drafted from the intake alone.* This is the one pattern where a phone call is worth more than any doctrine.

**Story must include:**
- The loss is acknowledged, not skipped over
- Feelings are named without judgment (sad, angry, missing, confused — all valid, all normal)
- The lost person / place / thing is *remembered specifically* — one small detail the character carries forward (the smell of grandma's kitchen, the way the old bedroom light came in in the morning)
- The story does not "fix" the loss. It ends with the character *carrying* it — softer, but still present.
- No moralizing about "everything happens for a reason" or "they're in a better place"
- A caregiver stays present through the whole story — grief is not held alone

**Parent-guide tool:** *The remembering ritual* — a small repeatable act the family does when the missing feeling comes up (light a candle, look at a photo, say the name out loud). Key phrase: *"what do you want to remember today?"*

## 9. Sharing / conflict with peers / "it's mine"

**Also phrased as:** doesn't share, snatches toys, screams when a friend takes something.

**Pattern:** Show the *feeling underneath* the grab — usually loss-of-control or fear-that-mine-won't-come-back. The story models negotiation ("I want it back at the end") rather than forced sharing. Ownership is respected; turns are chosen, not imposed.

**Age range:** 2–5.

**Reference:** `reference/storyverse/docs/07-therapeutic-techniques.md` — "Emotional Regulation" combined with `reference/azi-verse/future-ready-skills.md` — Empathy & Perspective-Taking.

**Template book:** No exact match in the library. Draft using the Moose's externalization for the grip-feeling and the Bramble arc's patient-kindness beat.

**Story must include:**
- The character wants to hold onto something — this want is not framed as bad
- The *feeling underneath* is named (the worry that the toy won't come back, the loss of control)
- A caregiver offers a *specific choice* instead of "share" — "you can play with it for five more minutes then it's [friend]'s turn"
- The turn happens; the toy comes back
- The character *notices* that the toy came back — the specific feeling of relief
- The friend is not made to be the bad guy; friends want turns too

**Parent-guide tool:** *The named turn* — a phrase for the actual moment. "You have five more minutes with the truck. Then it's Nadia's turn. And it will come back to you after." Key phrase: *"and it comes back."*

## 10. Self-doubt / "I can't" / performance worry

**Also phrased as:** gives up before trying, says "I'm bad at that", won't try new things.

**Pattern:** *You already know what to do* — a story where the character discovers they've had the skill all along, or where the caregiver names *specific evidence* the child hasn't noticed. The tool is not "believe in yourself"; it is *witness to specific competence*.

**Age range:** 4–8.

**Reference:** `reference/storyverse/docs/07-therapeutic-techniques.md` — "Anxiety Management → What's the Worst That Could Happen"; `reference/azi-verse/future-ready-skills.md` — Growth Mindset.

**Template book:** `content/originals/the_coocoo_and_the_boy_who_could_formatted.md` — the child discovers he already knows what to do at night; the house has always been his to hear.

**Story must include:**
- The character faces something that feels too big for them
- A moment of "I can't" — spoken out loud
- A caregiver / mentor lists *three specific past things* the character has already done that prove they have the piece they think they lack (this uses the intake's interests / traits as evidence)
- The character tries — and it works partially, not entirely. "Enough for today."
- The story ends with the character *doing the thing*, not with a lecture about believing in themselves

**Parent-guide tool:** *The three-things list* — the parent keeps a running list of specific small competencies to hand back when the child says "I can't". Key phrase: *"here's how I know you can."*

## Adaptation notes

- **Multiple sticky moments** — pick the *most active* one for the story spine. Others can appear as texture (the bedtime kid also has big feelings; that's fine — but one pattern is the arc).
- **No match** — pick the closest neighbour and log the adaptation in a `notes` field in the story.json + the buyer preview. Use the closest pattern's structural requirements as your starting point.
- **The child is older / younger than the pattern's range** — consult `reference/storyverse/docs/08-story-structure.md` for age-band structural specs (page count, sentence length, complexity). The *pattern* still applies; the *shape* changes.
- **New patterns worth adding** — as we do real orders and hit sticky moments not covered here, add entries. Every entry needs a template book (existing or newly authored) and a specific tool for the parent guide.

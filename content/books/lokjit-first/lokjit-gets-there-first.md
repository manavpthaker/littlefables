# Lokjit Gets There First

*For Lokjit, from Azad*

---

**1**

Lokjit's new room was very high up.

So high that the noise from the street had to climb all the way to the window, and by the time it got there it sounded like the sea.

---

**2**

Lokjit had always gone first.

First down the slide. First into the cold part of the pool. First to try the food nobody could name.

Azad went second. That was how they did it.

---

**3**

So when Lokjit went first this time, it made a certain amount of sense.

It was just a much longer way than the slide.

---

**4**

On the first morning he woke up early with about a hundred things to tell Azad.

The elevator that made his ears pop. The bird outside that argued with somebody every day at six. The trucks down below, small as shoes.

He called.

---

**5**

The screen came on.

"Azad?"

---

**6**

Azad had one question.

It was the same question he had asked every single morning for four years, and he had never once had to wait for the answer.

*What's in it?*

He meant the day. What's in the day.

---

**7**

He called at breakfast. He called after school. He called one more time to be completely sure.

Nothing. Nothing. Nothing.

---

**8**

"Is he *EVER* awake?" said Azad.

"Is he *EVER* awake?" said Lokjit, at the exact same second, from the other side of the world, about Azad.

---

**9**

So Azad stayed up late. He made it as far as the living room floor.

And Lokjit got up early. So early that it was still dark, and nobody else in the house thought it was a good idea.

---

**10**

Then Azad turned the hands on his toy clock all the way around to make it later.

Nothing happened.

The clock was not in charge of anything.

---

**11**

At Lokjit's new school, a boy looked over at his drawing.

"Who's that?"

"Azad," said Lokjit.

---

**12**

"Who's Azad?"

---

**13**

And Lokjit opened his mouth, and then closed it again.

Because how do you explain a person who has been there since you were four months old? Where would you even start?

He said, "My friend." Which was true, and nowhere near enough.

---

**14**

Back in New Jersey, Azad kept saying *we*.

*We* like the blue cups. *We* don't like the crunchy kind.

Nobody corrected him. He didn't notice he was doing it.

---

**15**

Then on Saturday, finally, they caught each other.

They talked about the elevator, and the loud bird, and a boy at Azad's school who could burp on purpose.

---

**16**

And right at the end, Lokjit said something small — the way you say a thing you're sure everybody already knows.

---

**17**

"It's Sunday here."

---

**18**

Azad sat all the way up.

"You already *did* Sunday?"

---

**19**

"The whole thing," said Lokjit. "It rained in the morning and then it stopped."

---

**20**

Azad looked out of his window.

Saturday. Blue sky. No rain anywhere.

Not yet.

---

**21**

And then Azad asked his question. The one he had been carrying around for a month with nowhere to put it.

"What's in it?"

And this time, somebody could answer.

---

**22**

Lokjit had not thought about it that way before.

He was not behind Azad's day.

He was in front of it.

---

**23**

The next morning it rained.

Then it stopped.

---

**24**

So this is how they do it now.

---

**25**

Lokjit calls at his bedtime, which is Azad's breakfast, because he has already been there.

"What's in it?"

"Wind," says Lokjit. "Wear the hood one."

---

**26**

And Azad calls at his bedtime, which is Lokjit's breakfast, to hand it back.

"How'd it go?"

"Good," says Azad. "There were a lot of leaves."

---

**27**

Lokjit takes the job seriously.

He knows what Tuesdays are like now. He warns about wind, and about assemblies, and about the days when the milk tastes funny.

Once he called just to say: "Don't wear the shoes with the buckles."

He would not explain why. He was usually right.

---

**28**

They send each other things, too.

Azad taught Lokjit three chords on the guitar. Lokjit taught Azad three taps with two fingers.

---

**29**

Then came a week when nobody answered anybody.

There was a birthday party. There was a long car ride with a nap in it.

Thursday went missing completely.

---

**30**

So Azad went to the window and asked the moon, who works nights, and sees both of them.

---

**31**

"He got up slow," said the moon. "He ate an egg. He put his shoes on the wrong feet and did not notice until the door."

The moon notices things like that. The moon has an excellent memory and nothing else to do.

---

**32**

"Anything else?"

"He drew something," said the moon. "He would not say what it was."

---

**33**

And on the other side of the world, Lokjit went to his window and asked the sun, who works days, and covers a great deal of ground.

---

**34**

"Fell off the low wall. Did not cry. Drew a truck," said the sun, already leaving. "Big day. Lot of children. Next."

"Wait," said Lokjit. "Was he okay?"

---

**35**

The sun stopped, which the sun does not like to do.

"He taps his fingers on things," it said. "Table. Bus window. His own knee. Three at a time. He doesn't seem to know he's doing it."

---

**36**

Lokjit knew exactly what that was.

He had given it to him.

---

**37**

Last night Azad went to the window to ask the moon how Lokjit's morning had gone.

"Ask him yourself," said the moon. "He's up."

---

**38**

So somewhere around the middle of the night, Azad hands over the day.

And Lokjit, who is already awake, takes it.

---

**39**

"What's in it?"

"How'd it go?"

---

**40**

Lokjit goes first.

He always has.

---

### The Rule

Whoever gets to the day first says what's in it.

Whoever finishes it says how it went.

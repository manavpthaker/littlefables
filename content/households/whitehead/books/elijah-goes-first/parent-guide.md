# Parent guide — Elijah Goes First

This is a birthday book, not a repair book. It was written to hand Elijah one
thing on the day he turned six: proof that being the smallest person in the
room and being the one who *goes* are not opposites. Kinley is in it as his
equal half, not his minder.

The story plants the idea. This page is how you use it on an ordinary Tuesday.

---

## The tool

### The Two-Part Power

Three steps. It takes about eight seconds and it works with two kids of
wildly different ages.

1. **Say what you see.** Out loud, before anybody moves. "The cup is going
   over." "He can't reach that." Naming it first is the whole trick — it turns
   a scramble into a plan. *This is Kinley's half. It gives the older child a
   real job that isn't "be in charge of your brother."*

2. **Say who goes.** One person moves. The other one holds still on purpose.
   "You go, I'll hold." *This is Elijah's half. Holding still is named as a
   job too, which is what keeps it from feeling like losing.*

3. **Name it after.** Give the thing they just did its hero-name. "That was
   the go." "That was longsight." *Specific praise attaches to a specific act.
   "Good job" evaporates; "that was the go" gets remembered and repeated.*

The point of splitting it in two is that neither child can do the whole thing
alone — which is true, and which is the part a six-year-old with a nine-year-old
sister does not always get told.

---

## Key phrases

- **"Who's got longsight?"** — Use it when something is about to go sideways
  and you want the noticing named instead of shouted. Usually Kinley. Sometimes,
  surprisingly, not.
- **"You see it, I go."** — The line from page 16. Say it when two kids are
  both lunging at the same job and neither is getting it done.
- **"That was the go."** — After the fact, for the moving half. The matching
  line for the seeing half is *"that was longsight."* Say them in front of other
  people when you can.

---

## In the moment

When one of them is being outrun by the other:

- "Hold up. Longsight first. What do you see?"
- "You see it. Who goes?"
- "Kinley — say it, don't do it. Let him go."
- "Elijah, you're the go. I'm holding."
- "Half and half. Both halves or neither."

When Elijah has gone quiet because something is too high, too fast, or too
big-kid:

- "That one's a go. You want it?"
- "Nobody's got longsight on this yet. Look first."

---

## Preventive use

Right before the situations where the six-year-old usually ends up watching —
a game with older cousins, the pool, a busy store, anything with a counter:

- "Before we go in — who's got longsight today, who's got the go?"
- "Elijah, if something needs somebody to move, that's yours."
- "Kinley, your job is telling him, not doing it for him."

Handing out the roles *before* the door opens is worth more than any
correction after.

---

## After the storm

When one of them ran the other over:

- **"I took your half."** — A repair sentence Kinley can borrow when she has
  done it for him again. Short, no shame in it, and it names the actual harm.
- **"I went without looking."** — Elijah's version, for when he moved before
  anybody saw anything.
- **"Nobody had longsight on that one. That's all that happened."** — What you
  say back. It takes the incident off either kid's character and puts it on the
  missing step.
- Then do the small physical thing — sit down together, split something,
  hands on the table. The book ends with a bath and rain on the roof for a
  reason. Land it somewhere soft.

---

*Half and half has been a whole thing this entire time.*

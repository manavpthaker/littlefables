# Parent guide — *One Step, Then Me*

This story is about the morning Averill stood in the door of the Coventry
fire station and did not want to go in, and then did.

It rehearses first. On page 4, out at the creek, Azi hops to a stone and
says the line where the worst outcome is a wet sock. On page 8, at the fire
station, he says **exactly the same line** with the stakes real. That is
deliberate, and it is how the tool is meant to work in your house too:
practise it somewhere small so it is already familiar when it matters.

It is built as a **ladder**, not a rescue. Nobody carried him in and nobody
told him it wasn't scary. Azi went one step, looked back, and waited. That
is the whole tool, and it is the reason the book ends with the fear still
intact and Averill inside the truck anyway.

---

## 1. The named tool — **The One-Step Way**

Three steps. It works on dogs, slides, deep water, the doctor, the vacuum,
the ocean, the big kid's birthday party.

1. **He picks the spot.** Before you go any closer, ask where he wants to
   stand. Wherever he says is correct. The doorway is correct. The car is
   correct.
2. **Someone he loves takes one step — and looks back.** One. Not three,
   not "come on". The looking back is the load-bearing part: it tells him
   the person going first has not left.
3. **He takes one step, when he takes it.** Then you stop again and start
   over from step one. Any step can be the last one that day.

The rule that makes it a tool rather than a trick: **stopping is a win.**
If he watches from the doorway for eleven minutes and then leaves, the
ladder worked. He got a rung.

**Sensory scaffolding.** Little bodies say "no" before mouths do. Watch for
the feet going heavy and planted, the fist closing around whatever he is
holding, the breath going high and shallow. Those are the signals to stop
advancing and go back to step one — not signs to encourage harder.

---

## 2. Key phrases

Three, and only three. They should sound the same every time so they become
family shorthand.

- **"How close do you want to be?"** — the opener. Hands him the dial.
- **"One step, then you."** — what the person going first says.
- **"Still big, huh."** — what you say afterward. Agrees with him. Does not
  argue the fear down or celebrate it away.

---

## 3. In the moment

When you are standing in front of the big thing and he has stopped.

- "You don't have to go in. We can look from right here."
- "How close do you want to be today?"
- "Want me to go first? I'll stay where you can see me."
- "One step. Then you — if you want."
- "You're holding my hand. I'm not going anywhere."
- *(when he stops)* "Good stopping. That's far enough for today."

Say the last one like you mean it, because it is true. Praise for stopping
is what keeps him willing to try the next rung next time.

---

## 4. Before it happens

Use these on the drive, or the night before — when there is no adrenaline
in the room.

- "Tomorrow we're going to see the fire trucks. They're really loud and
  really big. You can look from far away."
- "Where do you think you'll want to stand? By the door, or way back?"
- "If it's too much, we leave. That's allowed. Just tell me."

Naming the escape route in advance is most of the work. A child who knows
he can leave is far more likely to stay.

---

## 5. After the storm

For when it went badly — he cried, he refused, he had to be carried out,
or he melted down two hours later.

- "That was so big. I get why you didn't want to."
- "You watched the whole time. That's the hard part."
- "We can go back another day. Or not. Either one is fine."
- *(later, no lesson attached)* "Remember the yellow hoses hanging down?"
- *(or)* "Was it bigger than a monster truck?"

Do not review the failure or coach him for next time. Let the last thing
said about it be a warm detail, not an assessment.

---

## 6. Reading it aloud

- The short lines are breath marks. Let the page-five stop actually stop —
  a beat of silence where his feet get heavy does more than any adjective.
- Pages 4 and 8 are the same exchange twice. Say them the *same way* both
  times — same rhythm, same pitch. The repetition is the whole engagement
  device, and by the second one he will be waiting for it.
- Page 4 ends on a single word: **"Hop."** Land it, then turn the page.
- Page 9 is a crescendo — "Bigger. Bigger. BIGGER." Build it, then pause
  before turning. Page 10 is the reveal and it works on the turn.
- Give Azi and Averill different voices; the "Then me" is Averill's line and
  it wants to be small and decided.
- **Skungamaug** is a gift. Let him shout it. Tap it in the reader to hear
  it broken into Skun-ga-maug.
- The last page is a bedtime landing. Slow down, drop your volume, and
  don't add anything after "inside."
- If he wants to say "then me" with you, let him take the line. That is the
  book doing its job — the phrase moves from the page into his mouth, and
  from there into the next big thing.

---

## 7. Where else this ladder goes

The book is about a fire engine, but the One-Step Way is portable and it is
worth using somewhere low-stakes before you need it somewhere high-stakes.
Good rungs, roughly in order of difficulty:

- **Any stepping stone, kerb, or bottom step** — this is the page-4 version
  and it costs nothing. Hop one, look back, say the line. Do it often enough
  and the phrase is loaded before you ever need it.
- **The indoor playground** — the big-kid structure, the tall slide. He picks
  which level to climb to. You go up one and look back.
- **The splashpad** — the sprayer that goes off suddenly is a perfect ladder.
  Watch from the dry edge, then the wet edge, then one foot in.
- **The zoo** — the enclosures with genuinely enormous animals. Same shape as
  the horses on page 3: huge, and completely uninterested in him. That is the
  version of bigness that is safe to practise on.
- **Anything loud** — hand dryers, vacuums, the car wash.

Two notes specific to him:

**Monster trucks are the unit.** "Bigger than a monster truck" is on page 4
because that is the largest thing he has a word for. When you are standing in
front of something new and big, measuring it against monster trucks gives him
a way to size it up instead of just being flattened by it. "Is it bigger than
a monster truck? About the same? Smaller?"

**The little police car is not a security blanket to be weaned off.** He
carried it through the entire fire station and it appears on nine of ten
pages. A small controllable thing in the fist is a real regulation strategy,
not a habit to break. If he wants to bring a car to the zoo, let the car come
to the zoo.

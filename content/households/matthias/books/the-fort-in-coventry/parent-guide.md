# Parent guide — *The Fort in Coventry*

The story is a portal adventure: Averill and Azi build a fort in a house
too big for them, and every time they crawl out its other side they land
somewhere else on the farm — bigger, brighter, weirder — on a mission to
find a sleeping giant. The tool it hands your family is for the moment
your child is about to walk into somewhere new.

---

## The tool

**The Fort Radio.**

1. **Build a small enclosed space.** Cushions and a blanket over a
   chair, the deep alcove of a closet, a big cardboard box, even the
   corner of the couch with a throw over the top. Just tall enough that
   your kid can sit up inside. Warm, dim, safe. *The size of the space
   matters more than the aesthetics; being enclosed at child-height is
   the trick that switches the nervous system out of alert mode.*
2. **Put the lovey inside with the radio.** Averill's is a die-cast
   police car; your child's might be a bear, a stuffed dog, a plastic
   dinosaur. Whatever they already carry. The lovey has a "radio" —
   however you sell it. A tiny sound-effect. A pretend hand-cupped-to-
   ear. A whisper. *The radio is the externalization; it moves the
   threshold anxiety out of the child's chest and into an object they
   can operate.*
3. **Let the lovey report back.** Kid holds lovey to ear. Kid tells you
   what the radio said. Then — and only then — kid decides whether to
   go in. The lovey can go first. The lovey can come back and try
   again. The lovey can say "not today" and that is a valid answer.
   *This gives the child operator agency at the exact moment threshold
   anxiety would otherwise strip it away. The child is not walking in;
   they are dispatching a scout.*

---

## Key phrases

- **"What does the fort radio say?"** — the opener. Use it at any
  threshold: doorway of a birthday party, waiting room of a new
  doctor, first day of a new class, the front step of a friend's
  house they haven't been to before.
- **"Should we send the police car in first?"** — the deployment line.
  Names the lovey, gives the child the choice of scouting. Substitute
  whatever your child's lovey is.
- **"We know where it is now."** — the return line. What the boys say
  after the fire station reveal, and what closes each Fort Radio run.
  The point of the tool is not to fix the scary thing; it is to *know
  where it is*, which is what makes it manageable next time.

---

## In the moment

When your kid has planted at the threshold of a new place and won't go
in:

- "Where's your fort? Let's build one, right here."
- "The police car's radio is on. What is it saying?"
- "The car can go first. You tell me when it's back."
- "We just need to know where the giant is. We don't have to wake it."
- "Not today is a real answer. What did the radio find?"

---

## Preventive use

The night before, or the morning of, or the ten minutes in the car on
the way to a new place:

- "Let's do a fort radio run before we go. What do you think the car
  will hear?"
- "Tell me what your car is going to check on today."
- "Remember what the fort radio found at the fire station? What do
  you think it'll find here?"

---

## After the storm

If the threshold moment blew up (kid melted down at the door and
couldn't go in; kid went in and it was too much and had to leave), the
Fort Radio is exactly the tool for the post-mortem:

- "That was a hard door. What did the fort radio find?" — invites
  narration, not judgment.
- "Sometimes the radio says 'not today' after we've already gone in.
  That's the radio doing its job."
- "Should we build a fort tonight and check in on it? See what it's
  like now that we're home?"

---

## Real-world rungs

Places to run the Fort Radio in Averill's actual life — pulled from
the family's list of what he is currently into and where his edges
are, and deliberately kept out of the story text so they land here
where a parent can actually use them:

- **Movies at the theater** — the dark, the volume, the size of the
  screen. Fort Radio in the car park before you walk in.
- **The zoo** — big animals at close range are the fire engine
  problem, just with breath. Fort Radio at each new enclosure.
- **The splashpad and the indoor playground** — loud, crowded, other
  kids moving fast. Fort Radio at the entrance; the car scouts for
  where the calm corners are.
- **Doctor's office / dentist / haircut** — the classics. Fort Radio
  the night before, at home. Do a dry run.
- **A new classroom or a first day** — Fort Radio the morning of, in
  bed. Kid dispatches the car; car reports on what the room smells
  like, sounds like, who might be there.
- **Wrestling matches, monster-truck shows, live events** — anything
  loud and big and unfamiliar. Same drill.

---

## Rerun ritual

Nightly, at bedside, when the police car (or whatever the lovey is)
is on the pillow:

> **"What did the fort radio find today?"**

Kid tells you. Short, meandering, some invented, some real, doesn't
matter. This is the ritual that consolidates the day and preloads the
tool for tomorrow. Aim for once a week minimum; do not force it into
every bedtime.

---

*Some giants you find. Some giants you leave sleeping. Both are the
mission.*

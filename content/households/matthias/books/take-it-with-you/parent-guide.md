# For the grown-ups — how to use *Take It With You, Averill*

This is a book *and* a small toolkit. The story plants the seeds during
calm; the sections below are what to reach for in the actual moment.
Read this once when you get the book, then keep it near the reader.

---

## The tool: Take It With You

The book teaches a three-step move for the hard moment when a beloved
thing has to end and a small person's whole body says NO. It works for
screen time, but it also works for leaving the playground, closing the
LEGO project, or getting off the swings.

1. **Say the name.**
   Averill says the character's name out loud. Big voice. *"ZOOM!"*
   *This gives the beloved thing a shape that can travel. It also
   gives Averill something to do with the pent-up NO — a shout, not
   a scream.*

2. **Do the thing.**
   Averill does one thing the character does. A roar, a STOMP,
   arms-up wide, a kachow, a wrestler pose. Ten seconds is plenty.
   *This moves the intensity from the screen into the body — the
   feeling has somewhere to go besides "meltdown".*

3. **Pick where we go.**
   Averill picks the *next place*. Kitchen. Couch. Bath. Outside.
   *This is the transition. The character comes with him — so the
   move isn't a loss, it's a delivery.*

Three steps. In that order. Twenty seconds total when it's working.

---

## Key phrases

Two phrases become shared family vocabulary after a few reads. Say
them out loud, in Averill's Zoom-voice if you can.

- **"Take it with you."** — the whole tool in four words. When you say
  "screen off in one minute", follow up with *"and then we take it
  with us — where do we take Zoom?"* This is the pre-transition cue.
- **"The race track will still be there tomorrow."** — Zoom's line on
  the last page. Use it for the deep-breath moment after the screen
  is actually off, before the wobble starts. It's a permission line —
  he can leave the race without losing it.

## In the moment — when the meltdown is starting

Five things, all under ten words. Any one of them can steer the
moment. Pick one, don't try all five.

- **"Uh oh — where does Zoom go next?"** (deflects toward step 3)
- **"Let's take Zoom with us. Say the name."** (starts step 1)
- **"Roar first, then we go."** (permission for the STOMP; step 2 as
  a body-based release before the transition)
- **"You are wild AND you can come with me."** (names the trait,
  invites the move)
- **"Kitchen or couch, buddy? Zoom is coming."** (offers a choice
  inside the transition — the transition itself is not up for
  negotiation)

If the meltdown is already full-blown, none of these work. Skip to
"after the storm" below. That's fine. Gentle giants keep practicing.

---

## Preventive — right before you know the sticky moment is coming

Two scripts for the five-minute warning before you're about to end
the screen (or leave the playground, or close the toy).

- **"Five more minutes. Then we take it with us. Where should we take
  Zoom?"** — lets Averill *choose* the destination in advance, which
  is often enough to soften the actual transition.
- **"When the timer goes, Zoom is going to come find us. Where do you
  want him to find us?"** — recasts Zoom as the *agent of the
  transition*, not the victim of it. Zoom leaves the rug on purpose.

Set the actual timer visibly. Averill needs to see it counting down,
not just hear a promise.

---

## After the storm — when the tool didn't work fast enough

Sometimes the NO wins. That's normal. Repair, not shame.

- **"That was hard. The race was really good."** (validate the loss)
- **"Zoom didn't get to come with you that time. That's okay. He'll
  wait."** (Zoom is not judgy either)
- **"Want to try it now? Say the name. Do the thing. Pick where we
  go."** (offer the tool one more time, without pressure — sometimes
  the meltdown resolves and the tool works after)
- **"The race track will still be there tomorrow."** (a settling
  line for after, same one Zoom uses in the book)

Do not lecture, do not "next time we won't", do not "you should have
listened." Averill is 3. He is *learning*. The book gets read again
tomorrow. The tool gets tried again tomorrow. Every practice makes
the tool a little easier to reach.
